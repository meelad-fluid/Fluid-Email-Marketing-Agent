# The Fluid campaign playbook

_Source: Fluid's `fluid-campaign-strategist` skill (playbook.md), carried word for word except where it named Hiro or ClickUp._

This is Fluid Creatives' campaign and audience knowledge, carried inside this skill so a calendar can be built without any other skill installed. It is the same doctrine as the `fluid-email-expert` SOPs, narrowed to what actually decides a 30-day calendar and extended with the SMS guidance a calendar needs.

**How to use it:** read this *after* the evidence pull (`references/evidence.md`), not before. The data tells you what worked for this client; the playbook tells you what a healthy month looks like. Read together, they produce a calendar. Read in the wrong order, you anchor on the generic shape and then decorate it with numbers.

**When they disagree, the data wins for this client — but say that you're departing and why.** "Their education sends earn 2.4× the RPR of their promos, so I've gone 10 value / 2 promo instead of the usual 8/2" is an argument. Silently drifting from the playbook is just drift.

## The one idea that matters most

**Value-based beats discount-based, and most struggling programs are stuck in the discount cycle:** 20% off → 30% off → margins erode → ads get more expensive → revenue gets harder. The way out is campaigns that earn without a coupon.

The litmus test when you look at the last 90 days: **what share of sends needed a discount to work?** A healthy program is roughly **80% value-based, 20% sales/promo**. If the ratio is flipped, correcting it is probably the single most valuable thing this calendar does — and it should be stated as the headline of the plan, not buried in the slot mix.

This is the first thing to check in the evidence pull and the first thing to say when presenting.

## The monthly framework

**At least 10 campaigns per month.** The Fluid baseline structure:

- **8 value-based** — trust-building, product-focus, life tips — across the audience buckets
- **2 sales/promo** — direct offers, primarily to engaged

Once a month-one baseline is proven, stretch to 12–15, and layer seasonal/holiday campaigns on top of the regular cadence rather than replacing it. A 12-email request sits comfortably here; a 12-email month is a mature month, not an aggressive one.

The classic shape, useful as a starting grid before the data reshapes it:

| Week | Mon | Wed | Fri |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Value (education) | Product focus (best sellers) | Value (storytelling) |
| 2 | Value (problem-solving) | **SALES** | Value (how-to) |
| 3 | Product focus (new drop) | Value (tips) | Value (behind-the-scenes) |
| 4 | Value (lifestyle) | **SALES** | — |

Treat this as scaffolding. Real calendars bend around the client's actual send days, their promo windows, and whatever the topic clusters said.

## The three campaign types

Every campaign is one of three. A balanced month uses all three.

| Type | Purpose | Send to | Examples | Targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trust-building** | Build credibility, overcome objections | New subscribers, pre-purchase, lapsed | Us vs. them, reviews, press, myth-busting, brand story, social proof | 25–35% open / 5–10% click / 1–3% conv |
| **Product-focus** | Drive direct sales | Engaged, past purchasers, full list for major promos | Product highlights, collection drops, best sellers, back-in-stock, gift guides, new launches | 20–30% open / 8–15% click / 2–5% conv |
| **Value-based / life tips** | Genuine value, subtle product mention | All segments, especially engaged | How-tos, education, tips, problem-solving, industry insight, lifestyle, behind-the-scenes, ingredient deep-dives | 25–35% open / 10–20% click / 2–4% conv — **often the highest engagement and loyalty driver** |

## The nine non-coupon campaign types

These are the proven formats that earn without discounting. When the data doesn't dictate a slot, fill it from here rather than inventing a genre.

| # | Type | What it is | Frequency | Performance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Holiday** | Tie to a holiday, major or minor — even fun ones like National Pet Day | 2–3×/yr major, more for minor | 25–35% open / 2–4% conv |
| 2 | **How it's made / what's inside** | Process, ingredients, sourcing, manufacturing | 1×/quarter | 20–30% open / 2–3% conv, big loyalty lift |
| 3 | **Growth update** | Milestones, year in review, customer impact | 1×/quarter or annual | 25–35% open / 1–3% conv, very high loyalty + replies |
| 4 | **Single benefit focus** | One USP, one benefit, one feature — the *one* thing the brand does best | **1×/week** | 20–30% open / 10–18% click / 2–4% conv |
| 5 | **Back in stock** | Popular product returns — implies popularity and scarcity, no discount needed | 1×/month | 20–30% open / 12–20% click / 3–6% conv |
| 6 | **Product guide / how to use** | Styling, usage, routines, pairings | 1×/quarter | 25–35% open / 10–18% click / 2–4% conv |
| 7 | **Trending / best sellers** | Top sellers *with a seasonal angle* — "Fall Favorites," not "Best Sellers" | 2–3×/month | 20–30% open / 12–20% click / 3–6% conv |
| 8 | **New product drop** | A **four-email sequence**: teaser → drop → reminder → scarcity | Monthly or per launch | Drop 30–40% open; scarcity email 35–45%, the highest of the four |
| 9 | **Reviews / testimonials** | Featured customer reviews — pick *interesting or funny* ones, never boring ones | 1×/month | 25–35% open / 8–15% click / 2–4% conv |

Three things worth internalising:

**#4 is the workhorse.** Run it weekly. It forces clarity and is endlessly variable — every product has several benefits and every benefit is a send.

**Seasonal repackaging is free leverage.** "Best Sellers" becomes "Fall Favorites" → "Holiday Must-Haves" → "Spring Essentials." Same content, fresh feel, no new concept required.

**#8 is a sequence, not a slot.** If a launch lands in the window, it consumes four calendar slots, not one, and the scarcity email is the one that opens highest.

## Audiences: the Fluid allocation

Every campaign on a Fluid calendar serves one of three audiences, and the split is deliberate:

| Audience | Share of campaigns | Job | Shows up in the data as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Engaged** | **~80%** | Keep the list warm, drive repeat purchases, build the relationship | Repeat revenue |
| **Lapsed customers** | part of the remaining ~20% | Bring past buyers back before they're gone for good | Repeat revenue, from a dormant cohort |
| **Non-buyers** | part of the remaining ~20% | Convert people who joined the list and never purchased | **First-time revenue** |

**The 80% to engaged is the backbone and shouldn't be argued down.** These are the people opening your mail. They carry the value-based sends, the FAQs, the education, the product deep dives, the promos — the full range of topics. Volume here is what makes the program work, and cutting it to make room for clever targeting is the over-segmentation failure described below.

**The other two exist because the engaged list alone can't do everything.** Lapsed customers have bought before and stopped; nothing in the regular calendar speaks to that. Non-buyers are sitting on the list, reading, and never converting; the sends that work on a repeat customer often assume knowledge and trust a non-buyer hasn't built yet.

### Non-buyers deserve their own topics, not the engaged send forwarded on

This is the audience most calendars neglect, and it's the one that produces **first-time revenue** — so if a client wants growth rather than LTV, this is the lever. A non-buyer has never given you money, which means they have an unresolved objection. The topics that work address it directly:

- **Price objection** — "if it costs this little, is something wrong with it?" On Dolce this angle carried **29.1% first-time revenue against a 9.6% account average** — the highest-acquiring topic on the account.
- **Trust and credentials** — certifications, sourcing, manufacturing, what's actually in the product
- **Us vs. them** — the honest comparison against what they're using now
- **Reviews and social proof** — specifically the reviews that address hesitation, not the generic five-stars
- **First-order incentive** — a genuine reason to try, where a discount is most defensible
- **How it works / what to expect** — removing the friction of not knowing what they're buying

Trust-building is the dominant mode here. Aggressive selling to someone who has never bought converts badly and unsubscribes well.

### Lapsed customers

Past buyers who stopped. **Check for a winback flow before spending calendar slots here** — if one is live and earning, a lapsed campaign competes with an automation that already reaches them at a better moment. If there's no winback flow, or it isn't reporting, lapsed campaigns are genuinely uncovered inventory and worth two slots.

What works: a single specific reason to return (new product, something that changed, a restock of what they bought), not sentiment. "We miss you" is the weakest lapsed send there is. Discounts are more defensible here than anywhere else on the calendar.

### One overlap to get right, because it breaks the arithmetic

**"Engaged" and "non-buyer" are not mutually exclusive.** Someone can be opening every email and have never purchased — in fact that's the ideal non-buyer target. So a non-buyer campaign usually sends to *engaged **and** placed order 0 times*, not to a cold list of everyone who never bought.

Which means: **the 80/20 split is about what the send is written to do, not about which Klaviyo segment ID it goes to.** An objection-handling send to engaged non-buyers counts toward the non-buyer allocation even though its audience is a subset of engaged. If you count by segment ID instead, the numbers will look like 100% engaged and the distinction the calendar is making will vanish.

Never send a non-buyer campaign to unengaged non-buyers. That's the worst of both — no purchase intent and no engagement signal, and it costs deliverability.

### Building the audiences in Klaviyo

The planning split above says *who the send is for*. These are the segments that actually exist to send to:

| Segment | Definition | Typical size | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Engaged 90 Days** | Opened ≥1× in last 90d | 60–70% of list | **Default audience.** The 80%. |
| **Engaged non-buyers** | Engaged 90d AND placed order 0 times | varies | The non-buyer allocation |
| **Lapsed customers** | Bought 6+ months ago (or 90d for consumables), nothing since | 10–20% | The lapsed allocation |
| **Highly Engaged** | Opened ≥5× in 30d OR clicked ≥1× in 30d | 5–15% of engaged | Test new concepts here first; founder emails |
| **Window Shoppers** | Viewed/added/checkout-started in 30d, no purchase in 30d | 5–15% | High-intent non-buyers — fold into main sends or target directly |
| **VIP Customers** | ≥4 orders all-time (brand-adjustable) | 5–15% | Exclusives, early access, appreciation |
| **Unengaged 120 Days** | 0 opens in 120d, never purchased, not a recent signup | 10–20% | **Suppress.** Protects deliverability |

Check what segments this client has actually built (see `references/evidence.md`), and name real segments on the calendar — "Sent to: engaged" is a question the copywriter has to go ask someone. If the engaged-non-buyer segment doesn't exist yet, say so; it's a five-minute build and it unlocks the whole acquisition side.

**The 80/20 warning that governs all of this.** Over-segmentation costs more than under-segmentation. Twenty segments at 1–2 emails/week loses to five segments at 3–4 broad emails/week, badly — volume × relevance beats extreme relevance × low volume. Don't design a month that needs eight new audiences built before it can ship. Three audiences, one of which takes 80% of the volume, is the point.

**Frequency:**

| Send type | Audience | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Regular campaign | Engaged 90 Days | 3–4×/week |
| Non-buyer / objection | Engaged non-buyers | 1×/week, or folded into engaged sends |
| Win-back | Lapsed customers | 2×/month |
| Test / launch | Highly Engaged | 1×/week, then expand if it wins |
| VIP offer | VIP Customers | 2×/month |

## Cadence and frequency

- **Minimum 2×/week** — below this customers forget you, and it costs 40–50% of achievable revenue
- **Optimal 3–4×/week** — best balance of frequency and fatigue
- **Maximum 5–6×/week** — only when the content genuinely earns it; raises unsubscribe risk

**The frequency myth is worth pushing back on when it comes up.** "Sending too often gets us marked as spam" is not what happens. Spam complaints come from *irrelevance*, not frequency. Brands sending 3–4×/week see higher revenue per subscriber *and* lower unsubscribe rates among engaged customers, partly because disengaged people filter themselves out faster.

Twelve emails across 30 days is ~3×/week. That's the optimal band, not an aggressive ask.

## Plain text vs designed

Use both. Roughly **60–70% designed, 30–40% plain text**, and pick per campaign rather than by ratio.

| Designed | Plain text |
|---|---|
| Sales, promos, launches, back-in-stock, review roundups, multi-product sends | Value-based, storytelling, education, founder messages, behind-the-scenes, single-benefit |
| Branded look, visual hierarchy, clear button CTAs, drives clicks on product links | Higher deliverability, lands in primary tab more often, feels personal, higher open and reply rates |

**Design for sales, plain text for value.** Put the format in the ClickUp task's "Design Type" line — it's a real production input, not a stylistic note.

**A founder plain-text note needs a real reason to exist.** The format works because it reads like a person wrote it to you, and that collapses the moment the premise is invented. "Why I'm running this sale in September" is not a reason when the reason is obviously that it's Labor Day — the reader knows it's a holiday sale, so the note is pretending to explain something that needs no explanation, and it reads as a template. A founder note earns its slot when Jeff actually has something only he can say: why a batch turned out differently, what he saw at the farm, a decision he made and why, a mistake, a product he almost didn't launch, what a customer wrote in. **Before writing a plain-text note into a calendar, name the specific thing the founder is telling them.** If you can't, it's a designed email with a different angle.

## Sale windows: one email every single day

**When a sale is running, there is an email every day it runs. No gaps.** This is the highest-revenue-density stretch of any month — the offer is live, the audience knows it, and a dark day inside a sale window is revenue you chose not to collect. Normal cadence rules (3–4×/week) do not apply here; they govern the rest of the month.

The proven arc for a Friday-to-Monday sale:

**Day 1 — launch (Friday).** The sale goes live. Designed email. Lead with the reason to buy, not just the percentage.

**Day 2 — plain text (Saturday).** A personal, plain-text note. Quietest day of the weekend for inbox competition and the format that lands in the primary tab.

**Day 3 — reminder (Sunday).** The sale is still on. Designed. Different products or a different reason than Day 1 — not the launch email resent.

**Final day (Monday) — two touches.** An email in the **morning** saying it ends tonight, then an **SMS later in the day** as the last call. The closing email is consistently the highest earner of the whole arc, and the SMS catches the people who let the email sit.

Longer sales extend the middle: launch, plain text, then a reminder each day with a different hook — social proof, bestseller focus, an objection answered, a category — before the final-day close. **Never extend by repeating.** Every day needs its own reason to open, or the later sends just harvest unsubscribes.

Two things the data keeps confirming:

**The close massively outearns the open.** On one live Fluid account the Freedom Weekend arc ran kickoff $308 ($0.10 RPR) → founder plain text $4,695 ($0.77) → final hours $6,084 ($0.91) — the closing email out-earned the launch **9.1× on RPR**. So if a slot has to be cut, never cut the last day. And a weak launch number is not a failed sale; it's the normal shape.

**Don't widen the list for a sale.** The instinct is to blast everyone because the offer is good. The same account sent a stock-up promo to 10,955 and returned $0.13 RPR against a ~$0.50 norm on the engaged segment. Sales go to engaged, with recent purchasers suppressed on the later sends.

## SMS on the calendar

SMS is where most calendars get lazy: five SMS slots get filled with five shrunk-down versions of the emails. That wastes the channel and annoys the list.

**Place SMS at moments, not on a schedule.** SMS earns its keep on time-boxed, high-intent triggers where the value of arriving *now* is obvious:

| Moment | Why SMS |
|---|---|
| **Sale launch** | The window just opened; immediacy is the whole message |
| **Final hours / deadline** | The single best SMS use — the deadline *is* the content |
| **Back in stock / restock** | High intent, genuinely time-sensitive, pairs with a waitlist |
| **Launch or drop day** | Scarcity and first-access framing |
| **One non-promo value send** | Recipe, tip, customer story — keeps the list from being trained that SMS always means "buy" |

That last one matters more than it looks. A list that only ever hears from you during a sale becomes a list that only opens during a sale.

**Craft rules:**

- **One idea per message.** Email can carry three beats; SMS carries one. If the brief has three angle bullets, it's an email brief.
- **Pair, don't duplicate.** An SMS supporting an email should say the *shortest true version* of the same offer, not a summary of the email. Note the pairing in the task so the copywriter can see both.
- **Every SMS needs a link and a destination that matches it.** An SMS pointing at a generic homepage converts far worse than one pointing at the thing it just described — and broken or untagged links are the most common reason SMS revenue shows as $0 in analytics despite healthy click rates.
- **Respect the timing.** Business hours in the recipient's timezone, and put the send window in the brief ("9–11am ET"), not just the date.
- **Volume is low by design.** SMS lists are a fraction of email lists. Judge SMS on click rate and revenue per recipient, never on total revenue against email.

**A diagnostic worth running while you're in the data:** if SMS click rates are healthy (2–5%, typically several times the email click rate) but attributed revenue is near zero across multiple sends, that's an attribution or link problem, not an audience problem — and adding five more SMS on a broken link structure just produces five more zeros. Flag it before the calendar, because it's worth more than any slot on it.

## Subject lines

The brain stores the real subject line and preview text of every campaign, so read what this client's best senders said before giving direction. Give direction in the brief rather than writing the line; the copywriter writes it.

**What works:** under 50 characters; curiosity or specificity; personalize when it's natural, not as a tic.

Strong examples in the Fluid house style: *"5 ways to style our jeans"* · *"Our #1 best seller is back"* · *"We hit 500K followers!"* · *"This sold out in 48 hours"* · *"Your fall favorites are here"*

**Preview text** should complement the subject, not repeat it: *"And why everyone's buying it"* · *"See what customers are saying."*

**Trigger words to avoid.** Gmail's heuristic is "would a friend send this word to another friend?" Promotional language pushes mail to Promotions or spam.

- **High risk:** SALE (especially caps), FREE TRIAL, BUY NOW, OPEN NOW, LIMITED TIME, URGENT, ACT NOW, EXCLUSIVE OFFER, CLICK HERE, SHOP NOW
- **Medium risk, use carefully:** discount, offer, deal, promotion, special, save, off
- **Also:** all-caps words, multiple exclamation marks, excessive emoji, `$$$`, and anything misleading — misleading subjects generate complaints, which is the expensive kind of mistake

The conversational replacement is almost always better: "SALE: 50% OFF TODAY ONLY" → "Here's something we think you'll love."

## Testing, so next month has something to read

The reason a client ends up with zero subject-line evidence is that nothing was ever tested. Every calendar should leave the account better instrumented than it found it.

**Rules:** one variable at a time · minimum one week · minimum ~500 per variation · check significance before declaring · document the result · implement winners immediately.

Priorities, by expected impact:

| Element | Variations | Expected impact |
|---|---|---|
| Subject line | 2 | 5–15% open rate |
| Preview text | 2 | 3–10% open rate |
| CTA copy | 2 | 5–20% click rate |
| Discount amount | 10% / 15% / $10 | 10–30% conversion |
| Send timing | 30min / 1hr / 2hr / day | 5–15% conversion |
| Email length | short vs long | 5–10% conversion |
| Social proof | with vs without | 10–20% conversion |
| Plain text vs designed | format swap | highly variable |

**Set up 2–3 tests per month, not one per send.** Testing everything means learning nothing — with 12 sends and 12 tests you have no repeated conditions and no significance anywhere. Pick the two or three questions the last 90 days actually raised.

**A note on reading test results:** the winner flag in the analytics defaults to open rate. Open rate is the weakest of the available signals and, on accounts with Apple Mail privacy inflation (anything above ~60%), close to meaningless. Judge tests on revenue and revenue per recipient.

## Never running out of ideas

When a slot needs filling and the data doesn't dictate it:

1. **Start from pain points and desired states.** What's the customer's current state vs. the state they want? A fitness brand: low energy, slow progress, confusion → energy, faster results, confidence. Every gap between those two columns is a campaign.
2. **Mine the qualitative signal.** Customer reviews are the goldmine — people write their whole story in them: the problem, the switch, the result. Also competitor reviews, Instagram comments, Reddit, Facebook groups. This is where angles come from that the analytics can't show you.
3. **Sort into three buckets** — education (how-tos, tutorials, tips), storytelling (customer transformations, founder stories), problem-solving (pain point head-on, product as the answer).
4. **Keep a backlog of 20–50 ideas** so no calendar ever starts from a blank page.

## Benchmarks

Campaign-level, for judging what the evidence pull shows:

| Metric | Target | Reading it |
|---|---|---|
| Open rate | 20–30% | Below 20% → subject line or list health. **Above ~60% → Apple Mail inflation, not engagement** |
| Click rate | 8–15% | Below 8% → CTA or content problem |
| Conversion rate | 2–5% | Below 2% → offer or audience mismatch |
| Unsubscribe rate | <0.5% | Above → frequency too high or relevance too low |
| **Revenue per recipient** | **$0.50–$2.00** | **The single most important campaign metric** |
| Campaign share of email revenue | 25%+ | Below → campaigns underdeveloped, the program leans too hard on flows |

Benchmarks are a sanity check, not a target — compare a client to *their own* trailing average first. A $0.45 RPR account whose education cluster runs $0.72 has a clear instruction regardless of where the industry band sits.

## The audit checklist

Worth a pass over the last 90 days before building. Each of these, if present, is a finding that belongs in the presentation:

| Mistake | Revenue impact | Fix on the calendar |
|---|---|---|
| Only sending sales emails | ~50% revenue loss | Mix in 8 value-based per month |
| Not segmenting | 20–30% lower conversion | Build engaged / new / lapsed |
| Sending <2×/week | 40–50% revenue loss | Move to 3–4×/week |
| Generic subject lines | 20–30% lower open | Curiosity, specificity |
| No clear CTA | 30–40% lower click | One primary CTA, repeated |
| Boring review emails | ~50% lower engagement | Pick interesting, funny, surprising reviews |
| Several products competing in one email | Decision paralysis | One product or one clear theme per send |
| Constant discounting | Margin erosion | Shift to the nine non-coupon types |
| No A/B testing | Missed optimization, and no creative record | 2–3 tests/month |
| Ignoring the data | Repeat losses | This skill |
